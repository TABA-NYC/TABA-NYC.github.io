Building the AI assistant platform

Darwin's operations rely heavily on communication: prospects asking about homes, residents asking about maintenance and payments, all at varying times and time zones too. We had a third-party AI that handled part of it at $150K a year, and the leasing team could not keep up with the rest. We decided to build our own assistants instead. I led design of the platform: a builder where the team shapes each agent's rules, language, and knowledge, and the chat experiences those agents power on the marketing site and resident portal.

Context

Inquiries arrive at all hours and leasing teams are small. When we audited the funnel, thousands of inquiries had no follow-up notes at all, over a thousand had sat for more than a month untouched, and conversion from inquiry to signed lease ran below 3%. On the resident side, a third-party AI handled maintenance intake conversations while payment questions and everything else still landed on people. Every unanswered question, prospect or resident, was either lost revenue or a support escalation waiting to happen.

Process

We started with research and discovery: interviews, teardowns of other chat experiences, and a close look at agent builders like Sendbird. The Sendbird study earned its keep. Their builder pairs configuration with an interactive preview of the experience, and that immediate confirmation, seeing what the agent and the rules you defined would actually output, mattered enough that it became a requirement for ours.

Then we learned the operation before designing for it. A working session with one of the Ops managers walked us through how the team used Zendesk to capture communications across email and SMS. That conversation shaped two decisions: the knowledge the assistants draw from is the same documentation the Ops team already maintains, and escalation creates a ticket in the system the team already works in, not a new queue.

The pod's mandate framed everything: make AI features governable and controllable, so they empower operators without compromising quality. The builder is that mandate as an interface.

The Assistant Builder

The builder is where the team shapes an agent before it ever talks to anyone. Each assistant gets a persona, a greeting, and a standard operating procedure: a rules table written in plain language, like "identify the user's intent; if the query is suspicious or off-topic, do not reply directly." Behavior lives as legible rules the team can read and change, not prompts buried in code.

Each assistant also gets a toolbox: knowledge retrieval, policy and fee retrieval, availability and search, ticket escalation. Knowledge retrieval connects to real documentation sources with trigger prompts and a fallback message, so answers reference actual company policies and rules, including location-specific criteria, instead of generic model output.

Two details did the most work. A live test preview sits beside the configuration, so you talk to the assistant you are shaping before anyone else does. And every assistant carries version history with restore, so behavior changes are reviewable and reversible. Governed, auditable, undoable: that is what made operators comfortable putting agents in front of customers.

Agentic Chat

The profiles created in the builder power live conversations in two places: the marketing site, where the leasing assistant answers prospective residents about homes, and the resident portal, where agents handle maintenance requests, payment issues, and other escalations.

Escalation is a first-class path, not a failure state. The agent offers to create a support ticket, collects and validates contact information only when it does not already have it, and confirms what happens next. If ticket creation itself fails, the chat hands the person a direct submission link and a phone number. Nobody dead-ends.

The rest is trust furniture: a privacy and consent notice before the conversation starts, dated session history, explicit end-chat and start-new-chat controls, and a feedback prompt at the end of every conversation. Discoverability was designed rather than assumed: we specified widget placement, icon scale, entry animation, and the tooltip prompt against the marketing pages, and the old static leasing-resource link became a conversation.

How It Shipped

The builder went first: once the design passed review with the operations team lead it moved into development, and I worked directly with the engineering team to communicate the interactions. The agentic chat followed, running on the profiles the builder produced. Along the way I handed the chat design to Christina, a product designer on my team, so I could focus on the leasing inquiries project. The best-practices foundation I had built from earlier chatbot examples let her carry the use cases and edge cases we needed to account for, and I oversaw that work with Crystal, checking in and providing feedback when she was stuck. The assistants went on to absorb enough routine communication that operations held coverage through a 30% reduction in team size.

Reflection

The chat window is the visible part, but the builder was the real product. Giving operators legible rules, grounded knowledge, a test preview, and an undo button is what turned "an AI answers our customers" from a risk conversation into a rollout conversation. I would make the same trade again: spend the design effort where trust is created, and let the chat surface stay simple.
